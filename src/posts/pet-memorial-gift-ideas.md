Losing a pet is losing a family member. For many people it's a daily companion of ten or fifteen years, a creature who was there for moves, break-ups, new babies and quiet nights at home. Yet pet grief is often underestimated, and people can feel awkward about how to respond.

A thoughtful memorial gift says "I know this matters". This guide covers personal keepsake ideas, what to consider before giving one, and how to write a card when you don't know what to say.

## Before you choose a gift

**Timing matters.** Some people find comfort in a keepsake straight away; others need a few weeks before they can look at photos. If you're unsure, send a card or message now and a keepsake a little later.

**Keep it gentle.** You know the person best. Some people love bright, joyful reminders; others want something quiet and understated.

**Ask before using a photo.** If you plan a photo-based gift, it's often fine to find a picture on social media, but choose one the owner has shared themselves and seems to love.

**Don't push.** A gift shouldn't come with an expectation that they display it. Let them decide where it belongs.

## Memorial ideas with personal details

### A paw print keepsake

Paw prints are one of the most treasured memorial items. Some vet clinics and pet cremation services offer to take a clay or ink paw print; if the owner has one, a maker may be able to recreate it on a pendant, a framed print or a small plaque. If you have a nose print or paw print photo, ask the maker what format they need.

### A portrait from a favourite photo

A custom portrait, whether illustrated, painted, embroidered or printed, can capture a pet's personality in a way a snapshot sometimes doesn't. Look for:

- **A clear, well-lit photo** with the face in focus.
- **A proof stage**, so the colours and markings can be corrected before it's finished.
- **A neutral or simple background** if you're not sure of the owner's decor.

### A memorial ornament or hanging keepsake

A small ornament with the pet's name, dates and maybe a photo can hang on the Christmas tree each year, so the pet is still part of the season. This is especially meaningful if the loss is close to Christmas.

### A garden marker or memorial stone

For people who've buried a pet at home or have a favourite spot in the garden, a personalised stone or marker is a lovely tribute. In Australia's strong sun, ask whether the material, paint or print is UV-resistant and suitable outdoors, because many inks fade quickly in direct sun.

### A framed collar or tag display

Some people keep their pet's collar and tag but don't know what to do with them. A small shadow box frame with the collar, the tag and a photo is a simple, meaningful gift. You could offer to put it together with them, rather than taking the collar away.

### A photo book of their pet's life

If you have access to photos (or can ask family), a small photo book telling the pet's story is something the owner can return to over the years. Keep it short; 20 pages is plenty.

### A tree or plant to remember them by

A living plant can be comforting, especially one that suits the local climate. In south-east Queensland, hardy natives and frangipanis cope well with summer heat. Add a small personalised marker with the pet's name.

### A donation in their name

Some people would rather see something positive come from the loss. A donation to an animal rescue or shelter in the pet's name, with a card explaining it, is a gift that helps other animals.

## Ideas for children who've lost a pet

Children often grieve differently, sometimes with lots of questions and sometimes very little visible sadness. Hands-on projects can help:

- **A memory box** they can decorate and fill with photos, drawings and a favourite toy.
- **A drawing turned into a keepsake.** A child's drawing of their pet can be printed on a mug, a cushion or a framed print.
- **A storybook about pet loss**, chosen for their age. Ask a local librarian for suggestions.

## What to write in the card

Many people freeze when writing a sympathy card for a pet. Short, sincere and specific is best:

- "I'm so sorry about Max. He was such a gentle soul, and he always made me feel welcome when I came over."
- "Thinking of you. Bella was lucky to have you, and you were lucky to have her."
- "There's no such thing as 'just a pet'. I'm here if you want to talk about her."

If you have a memory of the pet, share it. Owners often love hearing that their pet meant something to other people too.

**Avoid:**

- "At least they're not suffering any more" (even if true, it can feel dismissive).
- "When will you get another one?"
- Comparing to your own pet loss in a way that shifts the focus.

## Practical notes on ordering

- **Allow time.** Custom portraits and engraved or printed items take time to make. If you'd like it to arrive by a particular date, such as Christmas, check production times and the [Australia Post Christmas sending dates](https://auspost.com.au/personal/sending/christmas-sending-dates).
- **Check spelling and dates.** Confirm the pet's name spelling and dates with a family member if you're not sure. A mistake on a memorial item hurts more than on any other gift.
- **Think about size.** Small keepsakes are easier to place than large frames.

## Caring for yourself if it's your own pet

If you're reading this because you've lost your own pet, I'm sorry. Grief for a pet is real grief. Talking to friends who understand, your vet, or a counselling service can help. Some people find making a small memorial themselves, such as planting something or putting together photos, is a helpful way to remember.

Whatever you choose, the most personal part of any memorial gift is the acknowledgement that a much-loved animal mattered.
