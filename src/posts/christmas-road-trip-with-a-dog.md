For plenty of Australian families, the Christmas break means a long drive: to the coast, to relatives in another town, or to a holiday rental somewhere cooler. Bringing the dog along can be easier and cheaper than arranging care, and many dogs are happier with their people than in a strange kennel.

It does take more planning than throwing a lead in the boot. Summer heat, busy highways and holiday-season bookings all add up. Here's a step-by-step plan, from the first booking to the first night away.

If you've weighed it up and the dog would be better off at home, the post on [arranging pet care over the break](/posts/christmas-pet-care-sitter-boarding-friend/) covers sitters, boarding and friends.

## Step 1: Be honest about whether your dog suits the trip

Some dogs love the car. Others drool, whine, or get carsick before you're out of the suburb. Before you book anything, think about:

- **How your dog handles car trips now.** A dog who struggles with twenty minutes will find six hours hard.
- **Age and health.** Very young, elderly, flat-faced (brachycephalic) and unwell dogs cope less well with heat and long days. A vet check-up beforehand is worth it.
- **What happens at the destination.** Will the dog be shut in a hot room while you're at the beach? Is there a secure yard? Will there be other dogs, small children or a busy household?

If your dog gets carsick or very anxious in the car, talk to your vet well before the trip. There are options, and they're better sorted out in November than on the morning you leave.

## Step 2: Book genuinely pet-friendly accommodation early

The summer holidays are peak season in most coastal and holiday towns, and pet-friendly places tend to book out. When you enquire, ask specific questions rather than relying on a "pets welcome" label:

- Is the yard fully fenced, and how high is the fence?
- Can the dog come inside, or must it stay outside?
- Can the dog be left alone in the property for a short time?
- Are there limits on size, breed or number of dogs, or extra cleaning fees?

Get the answers in writing, by email or through the booking platform, so there's no confusion at check-in.

Also check where your dog can actually go once you're there. **Dogs are generally not permitted in national parks** in most states, and many beaches have dog-on-leash, off-leash or no-dog zones that can change by time of day or season. The local council's website is the place to check dog-friendly beaches and parks.

## Step 3: Sort out ID, records and paperwork

Do this a week or two before you leave:

- [ ] **Check the microchip registration** is up to date with your current mobile number.
- [ ] **Check the ID tag** on the collar is legible and current. A holiday is a common time for dogs to go missing in unfamiliar places. The [pet tags guide](/posts/personalised-pet-tags-what-to-engrave/) covers what to put on one.
- [ ] **Vaccination and parasite treatments** are up to date. If you're heading somewhere with paralysis ticks (much of the east coast), ask your vet about prevention before you go.
- [ ] **Save a copy of your dog's vaccination record** and any medication details on your phone.
- [ ] **Look up a vet near your destination**, plus the nearest after-hours emergency clinic, and save both numbers.

## Step 4: Set up safe restraint in the car

A loose dog in a moving car is a risk to itself and everyone else. A dog can be thrown forward in a sudden stop, distract the driver, or bolt out of an open door at a servo. Under the Australian Road Rules, you can't drive with an animal on your lap, and road authorities in several states can fine drivers for an animal that isn't properly restrained. Check the current rules with your state's transport department.

Common options:

- **A car harness clipped to a seatbelt attachment.** Use a proper harness, never a collar, so the force isn't on the dog's neck.
- **A crate or travel carrier**, secured so it can't slide or tip.
- **A cargo barrier** for dogs riding in the back of a wagon or SUV.

Try whichever you choose on a couple of short drives beforehand, so the first time your dog wears it isn't the morning of a long trip.

## Step 5: Pack a dog bag

Keep it separate from your luggage so it's easy to grab:

- [ ] Food for the trip plus a couple of extra days, in the dog's usual brand (a sudden change can upset tummies)
- [ ] Water from home and a collapsible bowl
- [ ] Lead, spare lead and harness
- [ ] Poo bags, plenty of them
- [ ] Bedding or a blanket that smells like home
- [ ] Any medications, and the tick prevention you're using
- [ ] An old towel for wet or sandy paws
- [ ] A favourite toy or chew for the first night

## Step 6: Plan the drive around the heat

Summer is the hardest part. **Never leave a dog in a parked car**, even with the windows down, even "just for a minute". The temperature inside a car on a warm day climbs quickly and can be deadly. If a rest stop involves going into a shop, someone stays with the dog outside in the shade, or you take turns.

Some practical habits:

- **Leave early in the morning** and aim to arrive before the hottest part of the day.
- **Keep the air-conditioning running** where the dog sits, and check the air is actually reaching the back. Sunshades on the rear windows help.
- **Stop regularly**, roughly every couple of hours, for a short walk, a toilet break and water.
- **Check the ground at stops.** Bitumen and car-park asphalt get very hot. Try the back of your hand on it for a few seconds; if it's too hot for you, it's too hot for paws.
- **Feed a light meal** a few hours before you leave rather than a full breakfast, especially if your dog is prone to carsickness.

Keep an eye out for signs of heat stress at every stop: heavy panting that doesn't settle, thick drool, bright red or pale gums, wobbliness, vomiting or confusion. Heatstroke is an emergency. Get the dog somewhere cool, wet them with cool (not icy) water and head straight to the nearest vet. The [summer walking post](/posts/walking-the-dog-in-an-australian-summer/) has more on overheating signs.

## Step 7: Keep a firm hold at every stop

Rest stops and servos are where most escapes happen. A dog who's been in a car for two hours, in an unfamiliar spot, near a highway, is not the same dog as at home.

- Clip the lead on **before** you open the door.
- Open one door at a time.
- Keep the dog on lead at every stop, even if they're reliable off-lead at home.

## Step 8: Settle in on arrival

Before you unpack, walk the yard and check the fence line for gaps, loose palings and gates that don't latch. Holiday rentals are not always as secure as the listing suggests. Then:

- Set up the dog's bed, water and food in a quiet spot straight away.
- Keep the first evening low-key. A short walk and an early night help more than an exciting trip to the beach.
- Stick to the dog's normal feeding and walking times as closely as you can.
- Do a quick tick check each evening if you're anywhere near the bush or coast.

## Step 9: Plan the trip home, too

The drive home is often in the heat of the day, after a late checkout, with a car packed full. Plan it the same way: early start, regular stops, water, and no leaving the dog in the car. Once you're home, give your dog a quiet day or two to recover. Holidays are tiring for them too.
