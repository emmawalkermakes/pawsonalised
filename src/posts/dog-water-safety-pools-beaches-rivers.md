Water is one of the best ways to keep a dog cool and happy in summer. It's also where a lot of avoidable trouble happens. Dogs get tired in pools they can't climb out of, swallow too much salt water at the beach, and wade into creeks that look fine but aren't.

None of this means keeping your dog dry all summer. It means knowing what each kind of water asks of you. Here's a place-by-place guide.

## First, a reality check: can your dog actually swim?

Most dogs will paddle instinctively, but paddling isn't the same as swimming well. Some dogs struggle more than others:

- **Flat-faced breeds** such as pugs, French bulldogs and bulldogs often find it hard to keep their nose above water, and tire quickly.
- **Heavy, deep-chested or short-legged dogs** can sink faster than they can paddle.
- **Puppies, older dogs, and dogs with heart, breathing or joint problems** tire sooner and may not cope with cold water or waves.
- **Nervous first-timers** may panic and thrash rather than swim.

If you're not sure, start in shallow, calm water where your dog can stand, and let them choose how far to go. A well-fitted **dog life jacket** with a handle on the back is worth considering for weaker swimmers, boat trips and any dog still learning. Your vet can tell you whether swimming suits a dog with a health condition.

The main danger in a pool isn't getting in. It's getting out. A dog that falls or jumps in can swim laps along the smooth walls looking for a way out until it's exhausted.

What helps:

- **Teach the exit.** Guide your dog into the pool from the steps, swim them a short way out, then turn them back and guide them to the steps. Repeat over several short sessions until they head for the steps on their own. A bright pot plant or marker beside the steps can give them a visual landmark.
- **Consider a ramp** if your pool has no shallow steps. Pet pool ramps are made for exactly this.
- **Keep the pool fence closed.** Pool barrier rules exist to protect children, but a shut gate also keeps an unsupervised dog out.
- **Pool covers aren't a floor.** A dog can walk onto a soft cover, fall through or get tangled underneath it. Keep dogs off covered pools.
- **Never leave a dog swimming unsupervised,** even a confident one.
- **Rinse afterwards** with fresh water to wash off chlorine or salt, and discourage drinking from the pool. A full water bowl in the shade nearby helps.

**Know where dogs are allowed.** Councils set dog rules beach by beach, and many have off-leash areas, on-leash areas, time limits and dog-free zones, which can change over summer holidays. Check the council website or the signs at the beach access before you let your dog off lead.

**Salt water is not a drink.** Dogs that play fetch in the shallows can swallow a surprising amount of sea water. A bit may just cause vomiting or diarrhoea, but drinking a lot can make a dog seriously unwell. Bring fresh water and offer it often, take regular breaks out of the water, and if your dog is vomiting repeatedly, seems wobbly or unusually flat after a beach day, call a vet.

**Watch the waves and the rips.** Dogs get knocked over by dumping waves, and a rip can carry a dog out as easily as a person. Don't throw balls past the break, and keep play in the gentle shallows. Never swim out into rough water to rescue a dog; call for help instead. Many dogs will find their own way back to shore.

**Hot sand burns paws.** Dry sand in the middle of a summer day can be hot enough to hurt. Go early or late, and test the sand with the back of your hand.

**Bluebottles wash up in summer.** Dogs sniff and mouth them. Keep your dog away from anything blue on the tideline, and see a vet if your dog is stung and seems in pain, drools heavily or has swelling around the mouth.

## Rivers, creeks, lakes and dams

Fresh water looks gentle, but it's where some of the less obvious risks sit.

- **Blue-green algae.** Some algal blooms produce toxins that can kill dogs, sometimes quickly. Blooms are more likely in warm, still or slow-moving water. If the water looks green, scummy, like spilled paint, or smells bad, keep your dog out and stop them drinking it. Councils and water authorities often post algae warnings for lakes and dams, so check before you go. If your dog has been in suspect water and starts vomiting, drooling, trembling or collapsing, treat it as an emergency.
- **Currents after rain.** Summer storms can turn a quiet creek into fast, muddy water carrying debris. Skip swimming spots for a few days after heavy rain, and never let a dog into flood water.
- **Sharks and crocodiles.** In parts of Queensland, bull sharks live in rivers, estuaries and canals, and estuarine crocodiles are a real risk in the north of the state. Follow local warning signs, and don't let dogs swim or drink at the water's edge in croc country.
- **What's underfoot.** Snags, sharp rocks, broken glass and old fishing line are common. Check paws when you're done.
- **Mud and leeches.** Not dangerous in most cases, but check ears, between toes and the belly afterwards.

## Signs your dog has had enough

Swimming is hard work, and excited dogs don't always know when to stop. Bring them out for a rest if you notice:

- slowing down or paddling with the back end sinking
- head tilting back to keep the nose up
- frantic splashing with the front legs
- heavy panting or a dark red tongue once out of the water

Remember that a swim doesn't cancel out the heat. A wet dog lying in full sun can still overheat, so build in shade and rest time.

## After the swim: the five-minute routine

1. **Rinse** with fresh water, especially after salt water or a muddy dam.
2. **Dry the ears.** Water trapped in ears can contribute to ear infections in some dogs. A towel and a vet-recommended ear cleaner, if your vet suggests one, can help.
3. **Check paws and coat** for cuts, grass seeds, burrs and, in tick areas, ticks.
4. **Offer fresh water** and a shady spot to rest.
5. **Watch for anything unusual** over the next few hours: vomiting, coughing, lethargy or a swollen belly are all reasons to call the vet.

## Water sense in one paragraph

Start shallow, know your dog's limits, check the local rules and warnings, bring fresh drinking water, keep sessions short and supervised, and rinse and check afterwards. That covers most of what keeps a summer swim a good memory rather than an emergency vet visit.
