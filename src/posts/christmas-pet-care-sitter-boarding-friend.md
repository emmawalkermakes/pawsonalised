If you're going away over Christmas or New Year, the animal at home still needs ordinary days: food, water, medication if they take it, someone who notices if they seem off, and a plan for heat and storms. November is a sensible time to organise this. Popular dates get booked, and a proper conversation takes longer than a phone call the week you leave.

This is about arranging care while *you* are away. It is not about hosting guests in your own house — I wrote a separate [calm plan for Christmas visitors and pets](/posts/christmas-guests-and-pets-calm-plan/) — and it isn't about festive food, plants, gifts, memorials or tag engraving.

I'm a pet owner, not a vet or a professional sitter. Here's how I think through those three options, and how to hand over clearly enough that nobody is guessing.

## The three options, honestly

There isn't a single right answer — a long January trip is a different decision from three nights at the coast.

**A house-sitter or pet-sitter who comes to your home** keeps the animal in their own territory — the same bed, yard and street sounds. This often suits older pets, cats attached to their house, animals who find new places stressful, and anyone on a careful medication schedule. The trade-off is someone else in your house. Be comfortable with that, and choose someone you have reason to trust.

**Boarding** — a kennel, cattery, or other facility — means the animal goes somewhere set up for visiting pets. Some sociable dogs cope well, even enjoy it; for others, new smells and other animals are a lot. Boarding can be simpler if you don't want anyone in your home. Visit rather than booking on a website alone.

**A trusted friend or family member** can work beautifully, in your home or in theirs. They already know you, and often already know the animal. The catch is that Christmas is busy for them too. Someone who says yes in a rush and then juggles their own visitors may not manage the consistency your pet needs. Let them say no, and don't leave "keeping an eye on the dog" vague.

Some people mix these — a friend for a few days, a sitter for the rest. That's fine, as long as each person knows where their job starts and ends.

## What should actually drive the decision

I try not to start with what's convenient for me and squeeze the animal into it.

**Temperament.** Is your dog relaxed with new people, or do they take a week to warm up? Does your cat hide if the furniture moves? A confident, adaptable animal has more options than one who is shy, elderly, reactive or easily overwhelmed.

**Medication and special care.** Daily tablets, injections, a strict diet, or a condition that needs someone to notice small changes all argue for a person who can follow instructions. If the care is complicated, talk it through before you commit, and with your vet if you need the plan checked.

**Home or somewhere new.** Many cats do better at home. Some dogs do too. Other dogs are fine in a well-run boarding place. Think about your particular animal, not a general rule.

**How long you'll be away.** A single overnight is a different request from ten days over New Year. Short trips can work with a neighbour doing visits. Longer trips usually need real company and consistency, or a facility set up for it. And whether you'll be contactable: patchy reception is not the moment for vague instructions.

**House security if a sitter stays.** You're handing over keys, alarm codes, maybe the wifi password and the drawer where the spare lead lives. Treat that as seriously as any other house guest: references or a recommendation you trust, a conversation in person, and who else would come with them. If it feels rushed, keep looking.

## Questions worth asking

I'm not going to invent accreditation schemes or rules that may not apply to you. Satisfy yourself that the person or place can look after your animal.

For a sitter coming to your home, I would want to know:

- Have they cared for an animal like yours before — age, size, energy, species?
- Are they comfortable with the specific jobs: walks, medication, a litter tray, hiding a tablet in cheese?
- What happens if they get sick or can't continue? Is there a backup?
- Will anyone else be in the house with them?
- Can they provide references, and are they insured? Ask, then follow up yourself — don't take "she's great with dogs" from a stranger as the whole picture.
- How will they keep in touch — a daily text, a photo, a call if something's off?

For a boarding facility, visit if you can. A walk-through on an ordinary day tells you more than a brochure. Ask how animals are housed and exercised; what they need from you; how they handle an animal that isn't eating; who is there overnight; and what they do if a pet needs a vet. Ask how they manage heat and Queensland storms — shade, water, and a quieter spot for a nervous dog. I wrote separately about [walking a dog in an Australian summer](/posts/walking-the-dog-in-an-australian-summer/) and [storm season with a nervous dog](/posts/storm-season-nervous-dog-queensland/); those notes are useful to pass on.

Meet-and-greets matter. If a sitter and your dog don't get on on a calm Tuesday, Christmas Eve will not go better. If a facility won't let you look around, that itself is information. Ask friends more gently whether they can really do both ends of the day, and the messy bits as well as the cuddles.

## A written handover they can actually use

Verbal instructions evaporate in a tired, hot week. Write it down, leave a printed copy on the bench, and send the same text so they have it on their phone.

I include:

**Daily rhythm.** What time they eat, how much, which bowl, where the food lives. Treats they're allowed, and anything they are not — including leftovers from a sitter's Christmas lunch.

**Medication.** The name, the dose, the time, how you give it, and what to do if a dose is missed. Don't rely on "the usual tablet in the morning."

**Vet details.** Your regular clinic, its phone number, and the nearest after-hours emergency clinic, plus any ongoing conditions.

**Behaviour quirks.** The useful, slightly embarrassing stuff: they bark at the broom, they are frightened of men in hats, they will counter-surf if gravy is left out, they hide when the bin lorry comes.

**Where they like to sleep,** and whether doors should stay open or shut at night.

**Yard and house notes.** Which gate is unreliable. How the air conditioner works if you want it used. Where the lead, poo bags, litter and spare food live.

**Who to call.** You first, if you're reachable. Then a backup person who has a key or can decide if you're on a plane. Then the vet. Include mobiles, the house address, and any alarm instructions.

If something is urgent — this tablet is not optional, or do not let him off-lead at the park — put it at the top. If you can, have them do one walk, one feed or one overnight before the real trip, while you're still in town.

## Summer is part of the job

Australian Christmas care is summer care: heat, and in much of Queensland, storms.

Don't leave pets in a hot car, even "just while we duck in". Don't leave them in a yard with no shade, no water, or water sitting in full sun. A sitter who isn't used to our Decembers may not think this way automatically, so write it down: walk early or late, carry water, come home if the animal is struggling. The summer walking post above has more you can point them to.

If your dog is noise-sensitive, put storms in the handover: where they like to go, and that they should be inside before the weather hits, not after. The [storm season guide](/posts/storm-season-nervous-dog-queensland/) is written for owners, but a sitter can use the same ideas. If you're leaving a cat, closed-up rooms still need ventilation and fresh water, and a cat who suddenly stops eating is a reason to call the vet.

## Book the conversation, not just the dates

The point of starting in November isn't to panic. It's to give yourself time to meet someone, visit a place, write the handover, and change your mind if the first option isn't right. Those conversations are easier now than in mid-December.

Once it's arranged, do the unglamorous extras: update the number on the tag if it has changed, check the microchip registry, and leave enough food plus a bit extra.

Then go. The point is that you can leave without a running commentary of worry, and the person at home can look after a known individual rather than a set of guesses.
