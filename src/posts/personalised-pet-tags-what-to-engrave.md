A pet ID tag has one job: help a stranger get your pet back to you as quickly as possible. A lost dog in a thunderstorm or a cat that slipped out during a party won't wait for the finder to drive to a vet clinic to scan a microchip. A tag with the right information can mean a phone call within minutes.

Here's what to put on a personalised tag, what to leave off, and how to make sure it stays readable.

## Start with the legal basics (Brisbane and Queensland)

A tag is a backup, not a replacement for the official requirements. According to Brisbane City Council and the Queensland Government:

- **Microchipping is mandatory in Queensland.** Dogs and cats must be microchipped by the person selling or giving them away, and a puppy that isn't chipped must be microchipped before it's 12 weeks old.
- **Dogs in Brisbane must be registered with Council** from three months of age, or within 14 days of moving to Brisbane.
- **Council recommends your dog wears its registration tag at all times.**
- **Keep your microchip details current.** If you move or change numbers, you need to update your microchip registry as well as Council; Council doesn't update microchip records for you.

Rules differ between councils and states, so check your own council's website. Brisbane's are on the [Brisbane City Council dog registration page](https://www.brisbane.qld.gov.au/animals-and-pets/animal-registration-and-permits/register-your-pet).

## What to put on the tag

### 1. Your mobile number (the most important line)

If you only have room for one thing, make it a phone number that will actually be answered. A mobile is usually best. If you have space for two, add a partner's or family member's number.

Format it the way Australians read numbers, with spaces: **0400 000 000**. It's quicker to read and dial.

### 2. "Microchipped"

A short line like **MICROCHIPPED** or **I'M CHIPPED** tells a finder that any vet, shelter or council can scan the pet and find you, even if they can't reach you by phone. It can also discourage someone from keeping a found pet.

### 3. The pet's name (maybe)

This one is debated. Some people argue a name helps a finder calm and call a frightened pet. Others worry it makes it easier for someone to claim a pet that isn't theirs. Both views are reasonable.

A middle ground: put the name on the **back** of the tag, or leave it off and include it when the finder calls you.

### 4. Medical alerts

If your pet needs daily medication, has a serious allergy, is deaf or blind, or has a condition like diabetes or epilepsy, a short line such as **NEEDS MEDS** or **DEAF DOG** can change how a finder handles them and how urgently they call.

### 5. Suburb (optional)

A suburb gives a finder context ("this dog's from Ashgrove, it's wandered a long way"). For privacy, a suburb is usually enough; you don't need a street address.

## What to leave off

- **Your full street address.** It can be a privacy and security concern. A phone number is faster anyway.
- **Out-of-date numbers.** An old number is worse than no number. Order a new tag whenever you change phones or move.
- **Too much text.** Crowded tags get engraved or printed so small that they're unreadable, especially once scratched.
- **Jokes on the front.** "If I'm alone, I'm lost" is cute, but put the practical information first.
- **The word "REWARD"** is a personal choice. Some people like it; others worry about scams. If you use it, make sure the phone number is clear.

## A simple layout that works

**Front:**

```
BISCUIT
0400 000 000
```

**Back:**

```
MICROCHIPPED
ALT: 0411 111 111
```

Two to three short lines per side is plenty for most tags. Use the [tag text checker](/tag-text-checker/) to estimate whether your wording fits on your tag size.

## Choosing a tag that lasts

Tags take a beating: concrete, swimming, digging, and constant rubbing against the council tag. Some practical points:

- **Material:** stainless steel and brass are durable; aluminium is light but scratches more easily; printed or coated tags vary in how well they wear, so ask the seller how the text is applied and whether it's protected.
- **Method:** deep engraving lasts longer than shallow surface marks. Printed tags can be very legible and colourful, especially with a protective layer, but ask how they hold up to abrasion.
- **Size:** big enough to read, small enough to be comfortable. Small dogs and cats suit smaller, lighter tags.
- **Font:** simple capital letters in a clean font are easiest to read. Avoid fancy scripts for phone numbers.
- **Attachment:** a split ring can come loose; a sturdy S-hook clamped closed, or a slide-on tag threaded on the collar, can be more secure.

### Cats are different

Cats should wear **breakaway (quick-release) collars** so they don't get caught on fences or branches. Choose a light tag and accept that collars do occasionally get lost; that's exactly why the microchip matters.

## Check tags regularly

Make it a habit, perhaps when you renew registration each year, to:

1. Read the tag yourself. Is it still clear?
2. Call the number on it in your head. Is it still yours?
3. Check the ring or hook isn't worn thin.
4. Confirm your microchip registry details are up to date.

Before storm season and New Year's Eve fireworks is a particularly good time, since frightened pets are more likely to bolt.

## Quick checklist

- Mobile number, with spaces, on the front
- "Microchipped" on the tag
- Medical alert if needed
- Name and suburb optional
- No street address
- Simple capital letters, not too small
- Microchip and council details up to date

A good tag costs very little and might save you the worst night of your life. Ten minutes getting the wording right is well spent.
