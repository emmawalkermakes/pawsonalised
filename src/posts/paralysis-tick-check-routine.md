If you live anywhere along Australia's east coast, the paralysis tick (*Ixodes holocyclus*) is one of the most serious everyday risks to dogs and cats. Its toxin can affect the nerves and muscles, including those used for breathing and swallowing, and an affected pet can deteriorate quickly.

Prevention products from your vet are the first line of defence. A daily search with your hands is the second, and it costs nothing but five minutes. This post is a simple routine you can make part of the evening, plus what to do if you find a tick. It's general information, not a substitute for your vet's advice.

## When to check

Every day, if you live in or are visiting a tick area, which covers much of coastal Queensland and New South Wales and parts of eastern Victoria. Ticks are often talked about as a spring and summer problem, but in parts of Queensland they can turn up at any time of year. Be extra careful after bush walks, beach holidays, long grass or a visit to a property near bushland.

Ticks can be tiny when they first attach, about the size of a sesame seed, so you're feeling for a small bump as much as looking for one.

## The routine, in order

Doing it in the same order every time means you don't skip areas. Use your fingertips and move slowly, parting the fur as you go. Good light helps, and a torch on your phone is handy for long or dark coats.

1. **Head and face.** Most paralysis ticks are found from the shoulders forward. Check the muzzle, lips, chin, around the eyes, on top of the head, and both sides of the face.
2. **Ears.** Inside and outside the ear flaps, and the folds at the base of the ear.
3. **Neck and collar area.** Take the collar off. Ticks like to hide under it.
4. **Chest and front legs**, including the "armpits".
5. **Between the toes and pads** on all four feet.
6. **Back, sides and belly.**
7. **Back legs, groin and tail**, including under the tail.

If you find one tick, keep going. Where there's one, there can be others.

## If you find a tick

- **Stay calm and keep your pet calm.** Excitement and exertion don't help.
- **Remove it promptly** if you can do so safely. A tick-removal tool, or fine-tipped tweezers held close to the skin, works best. Avoid squeezing the tick's body.
- **Don't wait to see what happens.** Many vets recommend calling the clinic for advice as soon as you find a paralysis tick, even if your pet seems well. Signs can still appear, or get worse, after the tick is removed.
- **Keep the tick** in a sealed bag or jar so the vet can identify it.
- **Note the time** and where on the body you found it.
- **Keep your pet cool and quiet**, and watch closely for any of the signs below.

## The early signs of tick paralysis

Signs can be subtle at first. Watch for:

- Wobbliness or weakness, often in the back legs first
- A change in the sound of the bark or meow
- Coughing, gagging, retching or vomiting
- Laboured, noisy or rapid breathing
- Reluctance to eat, or difficulty swallowing
- Unusual tiredness or a change in behaviour

If you notice any of these, **treat it as an emergency** and go straight to your vet or the nearest emergency clinic, whether or not you've found a tick. Don't give food or water if your pet seems to have trouble swallowing, as it could be inhaled. Phone ahead so the clinic is ready.

## Prevention: a few notes

- **Talk to your vet** about which product suits your pet, their age and your area, and keep it up to date. Set a phone reminder if it's a monthly treatment.
- **Use cat products on cats only.** Some dog flea and tick products contain permethrin, which is highly toxic to cats. Never use a dog product on a cat, and be careful with cats who sleep close to a recently treated dog. Read the label, and ask your vet if you're unsure.
- **Keep grass short** and clear leaf litter in the yard, which gives ticks fewer places to wait.
- **Stick to paths** on walks rather than pushing through long grass and scrub.
- **No product is 100%.** Daily checks are still worth doing, even with prevention in place.

## Making it a habit

Tie the check to something that already happens every evening, like the post-dinner sit on the couch or the last trip outside before bed. Most dogs and many cats come to enjoy the attention, and you'll also get to know your pet's lumps, bumps and skin, which helps you spot other problems early.
