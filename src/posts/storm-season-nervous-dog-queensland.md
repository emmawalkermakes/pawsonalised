The first big storm of the season tends to arrive on a hot afternoon when you're not ready for it. The sky goes green-grey over the western suburbs, the wind picks up, and somewhere in the house a dog starts pacing, panting and trying to climb into the bathtub.

If that's your dog, you're not alone. Noise and storm fear is one of the most common behaviour problems vets and trainers see, and in south-east Queensland the storm season runs roughly from late spring right through summer. That's months of afternoons to get through, so it's worth having a plan rather than improvising each time.

I'm a dog owner, not a vet or a behaviourist. What follows is what I've learned living with a storm-phobic dog, plus the general advice vets and the RSPCA give. If your dog's fear is severe, your vet is the right first call, and there's a section on that at the end.

## First, know what fear looks like in your dog

Some dogs make it obvious. Others are quietly miserable. Signs to watch for, often well before you hear thunder:

- Panting when it isn't hot, or pacing between rooms
- Trembling, drooling, or yawning and lip-licking out of context
- Hiding: under beds, behind the toilet, in the shower recess
- Clinging to you, or the opposite — refusing to come out
- Digging, chewing door frames, or scratching at doors and fences
- Toileting inside when they normally wouldn't

Dogs often pick up a storm before we do. Changes in air pressure, wind and the static that builds before lightning are all thought to play a part, which is why some dogs start to worry while the sky still looks fine to you.

A useful exercise: next time a storm rolls in, jot down the time your dog first looked uneasy and what they did. After two or three storms you'll know your dog's early warning signs, and that's your cue to start the routine below.

## Build a storm spot before you need it

The single most helpful thing I did was give my dog somewhere to go that's *theirs*, set up on a calm day.

Dogs who are frightened usually look for a small, enclosed, darker space. Rather than fighting that instinct, work with it:

**Pick the quietest room.** An internal room with no windows (or with curtains you can close) is ideal. A laundry, walk-in robe or bathroom often works. If your dog already picks a hiding place, consider setting up there.

**Make it den-like.** A crate with a blanket draped over three sides, door left open, is a classic. No crate? A bed tucked between furniture or under a desk does the same job.

**Add sound and comfort.** Something to blur the noise helps — a fan, the radio, or calm music at a moderate volume. Add a well-worn T-shirt of yours, water, and a long-lasting chew.

**Practise on sunny days.** Feed the odd meal there. Toss treats in. Sit nearby with a book. You want the spot to mean "good things happen here", not "this is where I go when the sky explodes".

Never shut a panicking dog in a crate they aren't already comfortable in. Some dogs injure themselves trying to get out. The door stays open unless you've done the groundwork and know your dog settles there.

## Storm-day routine

Most Queensland storms are forecast hours ahead. The Bureau of Meteorology issues severe thunderstorm warnings, and the BOM app and website show radar, which is handy for checking whether that cell is heading your way.

When the forecast says storms are likely this afternoon:

1. **Walk early.** Get the main exercise and toilet stop done in the cooler morning, well before the build-up. A tired dog copes a little better than a wound-up one.
2. **Bring them in early.** Dogs who live outside should come inside before the storm starts, not during. Panicking dogs can clear fences they'd never normally attempt.
3. **Close up.** Curtains drawn, doors shut, gates checked. Turn on your noise-masking sound in the storm spot.
4. **Be calm and available.** The old idea that comforting a frightened dog "rewards the fear" has largely been set aside by behaviour experts. If your dog wants to lean on you, let them. Speak quietly, keep your own movements slow, and don't force them out of hiding.
5. **Offer something to do.** Some dogs can manage a lick mat, a stuffed chew toy or a snuffle mat, and licking and sniffing can be settling. If they won't eat, that tells you how stressed they are — don't push.
6. **Stay with them if you can.** If you know a big storm is due while you're at work, think about whether a neighbour, family member or dog sitter could be home.

## Escapes: the risk nobody plans for

Storm and fireworks nights are known for lost pets. Shelters and councils commonly see more stray dogs after big storms and New Year's Eve. A frightened dog can run a long way and end up somewhere unfamiliar.

So before the season really kicks in:

- **Check fences and gates** for gaps, loose palings and dig spots. Storm damage itself can open a gap, so check again afterwards.
- **Check ID.** Make sure your dog wears a collar with a readable tag showing at least your mobile number.
- **Update microchip details.** If you've moved or changed numbers, update the microchip registry. In Queensland, cats and dogs must generally be microchipped, and the chip is only useful if the details on it are current.
- **Take a recent photo** of your dog, showing their markings clearly. If they do go missing, you'll want it ready for local lost-pet groups and your council.

If your dog does get out, call your local council animal management team and nearby vets and shelters, and post in local lost-pet groups with that photo.

## After the storm

Take your dog out on lead for the first toilet stop afterwards. Branches may be down, gates may have blown open, and a still-jumpy dog can bolt at the next rumble.

Walk the yard. Look for fallen branches, broken glass, downed wires (stay well clear and report them to the energy provider) and anything sharp that's blown in. Storm water can carry rubbish and chemicals, so don't let your dog drink from puddles.

Then let them rest. Some dogs are flat and sleepy for the rest of the evening. That's normal.

## When to talk to your vet

Storm fear often gets worse over time rather than better, especially if each storm is a terrifying experience. Talk to your vet if:

- Your dog hurts themselves, damages doors or fences, or tries to escape
- The fear is spreading to other sounds (trucks, wind, the vacuum)
- It's taking hours for your dog to settle after a storm
- You're dreading every forecast

Vets can check for underlying pain or illness that can make noise sensitivity worse, discuss medication that some dogs need during the season, and refer you to a veterinary behaviourist for a training plan such as gradual desensitisation. Please don't give your dog any human medications to "calm them down". Some are dangerous to dogs, and only a vet can advise on a safe option and dose.

Products like snug-fitting anxiety wraps and pheromone diffusers help some dogs and do little for others. They're worth asking your vet about, but they're add-ons to a safe spot and a good routine, not a replacement.

- [ ] Note your dog's early warning signs next storm
- [ ] Set up and practise a storm spot
- [ ] Check fences, gates and dig spots
- [ ] Confirm the collar tag is readable and the microchip details are current
- [ ] Save your vet and nearest after-hours clinic in your phone
- [ ] Turn on BOM warnings for your area
- [ ] Book a vet chat if last season was rough

It won't make the thunder any quieter. But a dog with a safe spot and an owner who saw it coming has a much easier afternoon than one left to work it out alone.
