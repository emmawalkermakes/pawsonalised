The long summer break looks like the perfect time to bring home a new puppy or kitten. Everyone's around, there's time for toilet training, and nobody has to rush off to work.

It can work well. But the holidays also come with a few traps: a chaotic Christmas Day, visitors who all want a cuddle, travel plans, and a very sudden change in routine when everyone goes back to work and school in January. The plan below is about getting the good parts of a holiday arrival without the hangover.

## Before anything else: is it a gift?

Animal welfare organisations generally advise against giving a pet as a surprise present. A puppy or kitten is a commitment of many years, and the person who'll care for it should be part of the decision, choose the animal, and be ready for the cost and time.

If you'd like to give a pet as a gift within your own household, one common approach is to give the *idea* on Christmas Day: a lead, a bowl, a book about the breed, or a card promising a visit to the shelter or breeder together. The animal comes home later, when things are calm.

## Pick the arrival day carefully

The best day to bring a new pet home is a quiet one, ideally with a few more quiet days after it. That usually means **not** Christmas Eve, Christmas Day, Boxing Day or New Year's Eve.

Good timing looks like:

- **Several days at home afterwards**, with no big gatherings planned.
- **No travel in the first couple of weeks.** If you're going away over the break, consider bringing your pet home after you return.
- **A run-up to the return to work and school,** so there's time to practise the new routine (more on that below).
- **Avoiding New Year's Eve fireworks in the first few nights,** if you can. A frightened new arrival in an unfamiliar home is a hard start.

## Set up before they arrive

Getting the basics ready beforehand means the first day is spent settling in, not driving to the pet shop.

**For a puppy:**

- A crate or a small puppy-proofed area with a bed, which becomes their safe space
- Food that matches what the breeder or shelter has been feeding
- Food and water bowls, collar or harness, lead and an ID tag
- Puppy-safe chew toys
- Plenty of cleaning supplies and an enzyme-based cleaner for accidents
- Gates to block off stairs, pool areas and rooms you'd rather keep puppy-free

**For a kitten:**

- A quiet "base camp" room with litter tray, food, water, bed and a hiding spot
- The same litter type and food the kitten has been using
- A scratching post
- A sturdy carrier for vet trips
- Toys for supervised play (wand toys are great; string and ribbon should be put away afterwards)

Check the house at puppy or kitten height. Look for loose cables, small objects, toxic plants, open gaps behind appliances, and anything chewable you care about.

## The first two weeks, in stages

### Days 1 to 3: small world, quiet house

Keep things smaller than you think. A puppy should mainly be in their own area and the rooms where you are. A kitten should usually start in their base camp room and get to explore the rest of the house gradually over the following days.

Let the family know the house rules up front: no picking up a sleeping animal, no passing the new pet around, and gentle, short play sessions. Young animals sleep a lot, and they need it.

Take a puppy outside to the same toilet spot often, including after sleeping, eating and playing, and reward them calmly when they go there.

### Days 3 to 7: the vet, and settling a routine

Book a **vet check in the first week**, even if your puppy or kitten came with a health certificate. Take all the paperwork you were given. The vet can check general health, confirm the vaccination and parasite treatment schedule, and scan the microchip.

Ask your vet when it's safe for a puppy to walk in public areas and meet unfamiliar dogs. Puppies aren't fully protected until they've finished their vaccination course, so the advice is usually to keep them away from places where unknown dogs go until then. Socialisation can still happen safely in the meantime: carry them to see the world, invite calm, vaccinated dogs you know to visit, and introduce new sounds and surfaces at home.

Start building a simple daily shape: meals at roughly the same times, toilet trips, play, training, and rest.

### Days 7 to 14: introductions and paperwork

**Meeting resident pets** works best slowly. Swap bedding so they get used to each other's scent first. For cats, a door or screen between rooms lets them see each other without contact. For dogs, a first meeting on neutral ground, both on lead, is a common approach. Keep sessions short and end them while everyone is still calm.

**Meeting visitors** is fine in small doses. Ask guests to sit on the floor and let the puppy or kitten come to them, rather than reaching in.

**Sort out the admin:**

- Make sure the microchip registry has been transferred into your name and that your mobile number is right. Our guide to [checking and updating microchip details](/posts/check-update-pet-microchip-details/) walks through it.
- Check your council's requirements for registering a dog or cat, and the timeframe for new pets.
- Order an ID tag with a current phone number.

## Holiday hazards to watch for

New pets explore with their mouths. Over the festive season, keep these out of reach:

- **Christmas food**: chocolate, cooked bones, onion and garlic in leftovers, grapes, raisins and fruit cake.
- **Decorations**: tinsel and ribbon are especially risky for kittens, which may swallow them. Low baubles and fairy-light cables tempt puppies.
- **Wrapping and gift debris**: plastic ties, small toy parts and batteries.
- **Visitors' bags**, which may contain medication, chewing gum or snacks.

There's more on this in [our pet-safe Christmas questions guide](/posts/pet-safe-christmas-australia-questions/).

## The January problem, and how to avoid it

The biggest mistake with a holiday pet is spending every waking minute together for weeks, then suddenly leaving them alone all day when work starts again. That's a hard adjustment for any young animal.

Start practising normal life in the second week:

- **Short absences.** Leave the house for five minutes, then ten, then longer. Come and go calmly without a big fuss.
- **Alone time at home.** Let your puppy rest in their crate or area while you're in another room.
- **Your real schedule.** If you'll be leaving at 7.30am in January, start getting up and doing breakfast and a toilet trip at that time now.
- **A plan for long days.** Young puppies can't be left for a full working day. Work out who will come home at lunch, or whether a dog walker or puppy day care will help.

Kittens usually cope with alone time more easily than puppies, but they still need play, company and a predictable routine.

## A short checklist

- Decide together, and avoid surprise gifts.
- Choose a quiet arrival day, away from Christmas Day and New Year's Eve.
- Set up the space and supplies before they come home.
- Vet check in the first week; follow the vet's advice on vaccinations and outings.
- Keep the world small at first, then widen it gradually.
- Transfer the microchip, register with council and order a tag.
- Put Christmas food and decorations out of reach.
- Practise your January routine before January arrives.

Done this way, the summer break gives a new puppy or kitten exactly what they need: time, calm and people who are around to help them learn.
