Cats have a reputation for loving warmth, and in winter they'll follow a patch of sunlight across the floor all afternoon. A Queensland summer is a different matter. Days in the thirties, high humidity and hot nights can wear a cat down, and indoor cats rely completely on the house to stay comfortable.

Cats are good at hiding discomfort, so heat stress can creep up before you notice. Here's a look at a summer day through a cat's eyes, and what helps at each point. As always, your vet is the person to ask about your own cat, especially if they're elderly, overweight, unwell or a flat-faced breed like a Persian or British Shorthair, who cope less well with heat.

## Morning: the coolest part of the day

Early morning is when most cats are at their most active in summer, and it's a good time to shift the routine around them.

- **Play now, not at midday.** A short wand-toy session before the heat builds gives your cat exercise without risking overheating later.
- **Refresh the water.** Tip out yesterday's water, rinse the bowl and refill it with cool, fresh water.
- **Close the blinds on the sunny side** before the sun hits. Keeping the heat out is much easier than getting it out later.
- **Check the litter tray.** Hot weather makes trays smell faster, and some cats will avoid a smelly tray. Scooping more often helps.

## Late morning: setting up cool spots

By mid-morning, most cats start looking for somewhere cool to lie down. You can help them out by making sure good options exist in several rooms:

- **Hard floors.** Tiles, concrete and slate floors stay cooler than carpet. Bathrooms and laundries are often favourites for this reason.
- **Shade and airflow.** A spot near (but not right in front of) a fan, or a room with a ceiling fan running.
- **A cooling mat**, if your cat will use one. Some cats take to them and some ignore them completely. Supervise at first in case of chewing.
- **A damp towel** spread on the floor gives some cats another cool option.
- **Elevated beds** with mesh let air circulate underneath.

Cats are individuals. It's worth putting out a couple of options and watching which one they choose rather than deciding for them.

## Midday and early afternoon: the hottest hours

This is when it matters most that the house stays as cool as you can manage.

**Air-conditioning** helps a lot if you have it. Cats don't need it set cold; just taking the edge off the heat in one room makes a difference. Leave a door open to that room if your cat's free to choose.

**If you're going out**, think about what the house will be like at 2pm with the windows shut. Leave curtains drawn, leave a fan running in a safe spot if you're comfortable doing so, and make sure your cat can reach a cool room and water. A cat shut in a sunny bedroom on a hot afternoon can get into trouble quickly.

**Water, in more than one place.** Several bowls around the house mean your cat doesn't have to walk across a hot room to drink. Many cats prefer wide, shallow bowls (so their whiskers don't touch the sides) and some prefer moving water. A pet water fountain encourages some cats to drink more. Wet food also adds water to their diet, though any food left out in the heat should be cleared away before it spoils.

## The sunny windowsill problem

Indoor cats love a window seat, and that's where summer sun exposure happens. Cats with white or pale fur, especially on the ears and nose, are prone to sunburn, and repeated sun damage has been linked to skin cancer in cats. Australia's strong UV makes this a real issue, even through glass to some extent.

- Use blinds, sheer curtains or window film to cut down direct sun on a cat's favourite spot during the middle of the day.
- Offer a shaded alternative perch nearby.
- If you notice redness, scabbing or crusting on the ear tips or nose, see your vet. Ask them before using any sunscreen on a cat, as some ingredients in human sunscreens aren't safe for cats.

Catios and enclosed outdoor runs need shade, ventilation and water in summer too. A run that's fine in the morning can be dangerously hot by afternoon.

## Grooming in the heat

Regular brushing helps remove loose undercoat, which is especially helpful for long-haired cats in summer. Don't shave your cat without talking to your vet or a groomer first. A cat's coat provides some insulation and protection from the sun, and shaving isn't always the cooling fix it seems.

## Late afternoon: storms and power outages

Summer afternoons in south-east Queensland often bring storms, and with them, power outages. If the power goes off on a hot day, the fans and air-con go with it.

- Know which room in your house stays coolest without power, usually an interior bathroom or laundry with a tiled floor.
- Keep a couple of frozen water bottles in the freezer. Wrapped in a tea towel, they give a cat something cool to lie against.
- Keep the cat carrier somewhere easy to reach, in case you need to leave or get to the vet.

## Evening and night

Houses in Queensland can stay warm long after sunset, especially upstairs. Once it's cooler outside than in, open windows on opposite sides for a cross-breeze, as long as the screens are secure and your cat can't push them out. Falls from windows and balconies are a real risk for indoor cats, and a summer evening is exactly when windows get opened.

## Know the signs of heatstroke

Heatstroke is an emergency. Signs in cats can include:

- Panting or open-mouth breathing (cats rarely pant, so this is a warning sign)
- Restlessness, or seeking out cool surfaces frantically
- Drooling
- Red gums or tongue
- Vomiting or diarrhoea
- Weakness, stumbling or collapse

If you see these, move your cat somewhere cool, wet their fur with cool (not icy) water, and phone your vet or the nearest emergency clinic straight away while you get ready to go. Don't wait to see if it passes.
