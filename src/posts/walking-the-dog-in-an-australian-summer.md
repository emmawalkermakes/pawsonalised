Summer changes dog walking more than most owners expect. In spring it's easy: out the door whenever you like. By December in much of Australia, the pavement, the air and the timing all start to work against you. Dogs don't sweat the way we do. They cool themselves mainly by panting and, to a smaller degree, through their paw pads, so a walk that feels fine to you can be hard work for them.

This post is organised around a typical day, because that's how decisions actually come up: what to do at dawn, what to do at midday, and what to do when you get home. It's general information from an owner's point of view, not veterinary advice. Your own vet knows your dog's breed, age, coat and health, and is the right person to ask about limits for your particular animal.

## Before sunrise to mid-morning: the best window

The simplest summer rule is also the most useful: walk when it's coolest. In most places that means early morning, soon after sunrise, or late in the evening once the ground has had time to cool.

A few things to keep in mind:

- **Early starts help, but the ground stays warm in the evening.** Concrete, bitumen and sand hold heat for hours after the sun goes down. An evening walk can still be uncomfortable on paws if it's been a scorching day, so check the surface rather than assuming.
- **Shorter and more frequent is fine.** A brisk 20 minutes in the cool of the morning and a sniffy wander after dinner can be better than one long walk at the wrong time.
- **Adjust for the dog.** Older dogs, puppies, overweight dogs, dogs with thick or dark coats, and flat-faced breeds such as bulldogs, pugs and French bulldogs generally find heat harder. Flat-faced breeds in particular have a harder time breathing and cooling down, so they need extra care and often shorter outings.

Plan your route as well as your time. Choose shady streets, parks with trees, or a creek-side path where dogs are allowed, and avoid long stretches of open bitumen.

## The pavement test

Before you head out, especially after the sun has been up a while, do the simple hand test. Press the back of your hand firmly on the pavement for about five seconds. If it's too hot for you to keep it there comfortably, it's too hot for your dog's paws.

This is a rough guide rather than a precise measurement, but it's useful because it's free and takes seconds. Dark surfaces like asphalt, as well as artificial turf and metal surfaces such as drain covers and car park rails, heat up faster than grass or light-coloured concrete. Sand on beaches can also be scorching in the middle of the day.

If the surface fails the test:

- Walk on grass or dirt tracks instead.
- Keep the walk for another time.
- Do something indoors, like a short training session, a food puzzle or a game in the hallway.

Signs a dog's paws are suffering include licking or chewing the pads, limping, refusing to walk or lifting feet. If you see blisters, redness or raw skin, see your vet.

## Mid-morning to mid-afternoon: stay put

This is the hardest part of the day for most dogs, and generally the best time to skip outdoor exercise altogether. Some practical ideas for the middle of the day:

- **Keep the dog inside** with the fan or air-conditioning on, or in a cool, shaded part of the house.
- **Provide plenty of fresh water**, in more than one spot, and change it often. Dogs drink more in hot weather, and a spilled bowl in a hot afternoon is a real problem.
- **Offer cool surfaces.** Many dogs like lying on tiles. A cooling mat is an option; keep an eye on the dog to make sure it doesn't chew the mat.
- **Provide shade outside**, if the dog is out in the yard, and make sure it moves through the day. Shade shifts as the sun moves, and a spot that was cool at 10 am can be in full sun by 2 pm.
- **Never leave a dog in a parked car.** Even with the windows cracked open, a parked car can heat up very quickly, with fatal results. Leaving an animal in a vehicle in conditions that endanger it can also be an offence under state animal welfare laws. If you have to take the dog on an errand and can't bring it inside, leave it at home.

## What to carry on a walk

Even on a short walk, carrying a few things makes a difference:

- **Water and a collapsible bowl.** Offer small amounts regularly, rather than letting the dog gulp a lot all at once after running.
- **A towel or cloth** that you can wet to wipe the dog's chest and belly if needed.
- **Poo bags**, naturally.
- **A well-fitted ID tag**, with your current mobile number. Dogs panicking from heat or storms can bolt, and summer storms and fireworks can also startle them. If you want to know what to put on a tag, our post on [what to engrave on a pet tag](/posts/personalised-pet-tags-what-to-engrave/) goes through it.
- **A light-weight lead** and a harness or a collar that fits correctly. Tight collars and heavy gear make panting harder.

If you're heading somewhere remote, bring more water than you think you'll need, and tell someone where you're going.

## Know the signs of overheating

Heat stress in dogs can become an emergency quickly, so it pays to know what to look for. Common warning signs include:

- Heavy, noisy or rapid panting that doesn't ease when the dog rests
- Drooling more than usual, or thick, sticky saliva
- Bright red or very pale gums
- Weakness, stumbling or lagging behind
- Vomiting or diarrhoea
- Collapse or seizures

If you see early signs, stop, move to shade or an air-conditioned space, offer small sips of water, and cool the dog gradually, for example by wetting the body with cool (not ice-cold) water. If the signs are serious or don't improve quickly, call a vet straight away. Some clinics have emergency or after-hours numbers, so it's worth saving yours in your phone now, before you need it.

Avoid ice baths, which can cause other problems. Your vet can give detailed first-aid advice for heat stress, so ask at your next visit.

## Late afternoon to night: easing back out

The evening can be a good time for a second outing, but hold off until the pavement test passes and the air has started to cool. A few extras to consider:

- **Insects.** Mosquitoes tend to be more active around dusk, so check with your vet about appropriate protection for your dog, especially for heartworm prevention, which is a year-round conversation in many areas.
- **Storms.** Afternoon storms are common in summer in many regions. Some dogs are terrified of thunder. If yours is, keep it indoors and make the space as calm as you can.
- **Snakes.** In warmer months snakes are more active in many parts of Australia. Keep your dog on a lead near long grass and scrub, and ask your vet what to do in an emergency.
- **Water hazards.** Blue-green algae can bloom in still water in warm weather and is dangerous for dogs. Take notice of local warning signs and don't let your dog swim or drink from water that looks scummy or green.

## What if you can't walk much?

On the hottest days it's okay to skip the walk. Dogs don't need a lap of the block to be happy. You can keep your dog busy and tired with:

- A short training session indoors, practising a few cues for treats
- A snuffle mat or scattering the dinner in the grass
- A frozen treat, such as a lick mat with a smear of plain yoghurt, if your dog tolerates it (check which foods are safe with your vet)

## A simple summer routine, summed up

- **Early morning:** short, shaded walk, pavement test first
- **Mid-morning to afternoon:** inside, water, cool spot, enrichment
- **Early evening:** second short outing if the ground has cooled
- **Always:** water, an ID tag with a current number, and a plan for what to do if your dog shows signs of heat stress

Summer walks can be a pleasure, as long as the timing, the ground and the dog's condition all line up. When they don't, a bit of patience and a different kind of activity are a perfectly good swap.
