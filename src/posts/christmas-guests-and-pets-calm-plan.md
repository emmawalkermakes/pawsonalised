Christmas week in Australia is loud, hot and full of people who love your pets — and don't always know your house rules. Gates get left open. Kids rush in for a cuddle. Someone puts a platter of ham on the coffee table. Somewhere between the airport run and the pavlova, the dog is under everyone's feet and the cat has vanished under the bed.

This is a calm, practical plan for hosting visitors when you have pets. I'm a pet owner, not a vet. Nothing here replaces advice from your own vet if your animal is anxious, reactive or unwell.

## The week-before checklist

Do these while the house is still quiet. Ten minutes a day beats a scramble on Christmas Eve.

**ID and contacts**

- [ ] Check that collars fit and **ID tags** are readable (name + your mobile).
- [ ] Confirm microchip details are up to date with your current phone number and address.
- [ ] Save your regular vet and the nearest **after-hours emergency clinic** in your phone, and on a sticky note on the fridge for guests.
- [ ] If your pet wears a GPS or Bluetooth tracker, charge it and test it.

**House setup**

- [ ] Choose a **quiet room** (spare bedroom, study, or laundry with a bed and water) that can be shut when the house peaks.
- [ ] Put a baby gate or keep a door that latches — something guests can't accidentally leave wide open.
- [ ] Move breakable decorations and food bowls out of the main traffic path.
- [ ] Decide where leads, keys and the "dog coming through" zone will live near the front door.

**People briefing**

- [ ] Text overnight guests your two or three non-negotiables (for example: "Please don't feed the dog from the table" and "Front door stays shut until the dog is held or in their room").
- [ ] Ask who is bringing kids, and whether any guest is nervous around dogs or allergic to cats.
- [ ] If someone wants to bring their own dog, decide **before** they arrive — not on the doorstep.

## Door routine: stop the bolt before it starts

Most Christmas escapes happen in the first thirty seconds of a greeting. Bags, hugs, kids and a ringing doorbell are a lot for a dog that already thinks visitors are the best day of the year.

Pick a simple routine and stick to it:

1. **Phone alert or doorbell** → one adult is "on door duty."
2. Dog goes on a lead, behind a gate, or into the quiet room **before** the door opens.
3. Guests come in, put bags down, close the door.
4. Only then: calm hello, or a short lead greeting in the hallway.

Put a handwritten note on the inside of the front door if your household is chaotic: *Dog first — then door.* It sounds fussy. It prevents a sprint down the street on a forty-degree afternoon.

For cats, the same idea applies in reverse: open the door wide only when you know where the cat is. Many cats bolt toward a gap when the house suddenly fills with strangers. A closed quiet room with litter, water and a hidey box is kinder than "they'll be fine under the couch."

## Kids and pets: a two-minute briefing at the door

Children are often the keenest guests and the least predictable. You don't need a lecture — you need a clear script adults can repeat.

Try something like this when kids arrive:

- Pat the **shoulder or chest**, not the face or tail.
- If the dog walks away, **let them go** — don't follow.
- No climbing on the dog, no ears pulled, no food held above the head as a game.
- Cat time is invitation-only: if the cat is in their room or high up, we leave them alone.

Supervise the first meeting even with a "good with kids" dog. Christmas excitement changes everyone's behaviour. Give the dog a chew or a stuffed toy in their quiet space so they have a job that isn't entertaining the whole extended family.

## Food on tables: the Christmas hazard map

Australian Christmas food lives at nose height — platters on low tables, grazing boards, leftover ham on the barbecue trolley. You don't need to ban food from the living room; you need a plan.

**High-risk leftovers to clear quickly:** cooked bones, corn cobs, kebab skewers, chocolate, grapes/raisins/sultanas (pudding, mince pies), macadamias, onion-heavy dips, and anything with xylitol. If you're unsure about a food, keep it for humans only.

**Practical hosting tricks**

- Assign one person (or rotate) as **plate clearer** after each round of snacks.
- Use higher surfaces for chocolate and dessert boards.
- Give the dog their own long-lasting chew **before** guests eat, so they're busy rather than begging.
- Tell well-meaning relatives your rule once, warmly, and then enforce it: "We've got his dinner sorted — please don't sneak him ham."

If a pet eats something worrying, call the vet or emergency clinic promptly. Don't wait to "see how they go" with chocolate, raisins, xylitol or cooked bones.

## Noise: storms, fireworks and a full house

December in much of Australia means afternoon storms, plus New Year fireworks in many suburbs and the general roar of a full house. Animals that cope fine in October can struggle when the fridge door never shuts and chairs scrape all day.

**Before the busy days**

- Practise short periods in the quiet room with the fan or radio on, so it isn't only associated with "everyone left me."
- Check that your pet's anxiety plan (if you have one from the vet) is stocked — never start a new medication on Christmas morning without veterinary advice.
- Note local fireworks timing if your council or city publishes it, so you can plan a walk earlier and be home before the bangs.

**On the day**

- Let the pet retreat. Closing the quiet-room door is not unkind; forced socialising is.
- Skip the "they'll get used to it if we keep them in the lounge" approach for genuinely scared animals.
- Keep leads and carriers easy to grab if you need to move rooms quickly during a storm.

## A day-of timeline (adapt it)

Use this as a skeleton for Christmas Day or any big visiting day.

| Time | People | Pets |
|---|---|---|
| Early morning | Prep food, clear surfaces | Walk or play while it's cooler; normal breakfast |
| Mid-morning | First guests | Door routine on; quiet room ready with water |
| Lunch service | Food out, kids everywhere | Dog on settle/chew or in quiet room during peak plate chaos |
| Afternoon lull | Naps, leftover grazing | Another short toilet break; check water; reset the quiet room |
| Evening | More visitors or fireworks risk | Earlier dinner; lights on in quiet room; avoid last-minute long walks in the heat |

The exact clock times matter less than the pattern: **exercise and toilet breaks before the house peaks**, and a retreat option that guests understand is off-limits without you.

## Overnight guests

If people are staying:

- Show them the quiet room and the door rule on arrival, not at midnight.
- Tell them whether the dog is allowed in guest rooms (many aren't, and guests don't know).
- Ask smokers and late-night snackers not to prop the back door open.
- If your cat usually goes out at night, decide whether that's still safe with unfamiliar people moving through the house — many owners keep cats in for peak guest nights.

## When to scale the gathering down

A calm plan still has limits. If your dog is reactive at the door, recovering from surgery, or deeply noise-phobic, it's reasonable to:

- Host fewer people at your place and meet others at a park or relative's house without pets.
- Board or book a trusted pet sitter for the loudest evening (New Year's Eve is a common choice).
- Ask one visitor-heavy meal to happen elsewhere.

Looking after your animal is part of being a good host, not a failure of hospitality.

## Quick copy-paste text for guests

Feel free to send something like this the day before:

> Looking forward to seeing you! Quick pet notes so everyone stays calm: please wait for us to open the door (we'll put Milo on a lead first), no feeding from the table, and the study is his quiet room if he needs a break. Kids are welcome to pat him when he's settled — we'll show them how. Thanks!

Short, kind, specific. Most guests are relieved to be told what to do.

*Not veterinary advice. If your pet is distressed, injured or has eaten something harmful, contact your vet or a 24-hour emergency clinic.*
