Christmas in Australia is a strange mix for pets. There's more food on low tables than at any other time of year, a house full of visitors leaving gates open, decorations at nose height, and it all happens in the hottest, stormiest part of the year, often finished off with fireworks.

Rather than another list of "festive hazards", I've written this as the questions I hear from friends and family every December, with the straightest answers I can give. I'm a pet owner, not a vet, so where something is urgent the answer is always the same: **ring your vet or the nearest 24-hour emergency vet straight away**. Save that number in your phone today, while you're calm.

## Food questions

### Can the dog have a bit of Christmas ham?

It's not poisonous, but it's a bad idea. Ham is very salty and fatty, and rich, fatty food is a well-known trigger for pancreatitis in dogs, which is painful and can need hospital care. The fat trimmings everyone wants to "not waste" are the worst part. If you want the dog included, put aside a few pieces of plain cooked meat with no skin, fat, gravy or seasoning.

### What about the bones?

No cooked bones, ever. Cooked bones (ham bone, chicken carcass, the leftover lamb leg) can splinter and cause choking, broken teeth or blockages in the gut. Bag the carcass and put it straight in an outside bin with a closed lid, because a determined dog will raid the kitchen bin.

### Which Christmas foods are actually toxic?

The ones that turn up on Australian tables most at Christmas:

- **Chocolate**, including chocolate-coated anything and the advent calendar. Dark and cooking chocolate are the most dangerous.
- **Grapes, raisins, sultanas and currants.** That means Christmas pudding, fruit cake, fruit mince pies and panettone. These can cause kidney damage in dogs, and the amount that causes trouble varies a lot between dogs, so there's no "safe" small amount.
- **Macadamia nuts**, which are toxic to dogs and turn up in gift tins, shortbread and home-baked biscuits.
- **Onions and garlic**, in any form, including stuffing, gravy and many dips. These are a problem for both dogs and cats.
- **Xylitol**, an artificial sweetener in some sugar-free lollies, gum, baked goods and some peanut butters. It's very dangerous to dogs. Check labels before using peanut butter in a treat toy.
- **Alcohol**, including the half-finished drinks left on the coffee table at the end of the night.

If your pet eats any of these, don't wait for symptoms. Ring the vet, tell them what was eaten, roughly how much, and your pet's weight.

### Is a corn cob okay to chew on?

No. Corn on the cob is a barbecue staple, and a dog that swallows a chunk of cob can end up with a blockage that needs surgery. The same goes for kebab skewers that still smell like meat. Clear the barbecue plates before anyone sits down to chat.

## Decoration questions

### Are Christmas lilies a problem?

For cats, yes, a serious one. True lilies (*Lilium* species, including the Christmas lily sold in Australia in December) and day lilies are highly toxic to cats. Even a small amount of pollen groomed off their fur, or water from the vase, can cause kidney failure. If you have a cat, the simplest answer is not to have lilies in the house, and to tell well-meaning visitors before they arrive with a bunch.

### My cat is obsessed with tinsel. Should I worry?

Yes. Tinsel, curling ribbon and string are the kind of thing vets worry about with cats: a long, thin object that's swallowed can bunch up the intestine and needs urgent surgery. If your cat plays with tinsel, skip it this year and use paper or fabric decorations instead. Pick up ribbon as soon as presents are opened.

### Do I need to dog-proof the tree?

A bit. The common problems are:

- **Low-hanging glass baubles** that get knocked off and broken.
- **Edible decorations**, like chocolate ornaments or candy canes, which a dog will find.
- **Fairy light cables** for puppies and rabbits who chew.
- **Presents under the tree**, especially anything containing food. Dogs can smell a box of chocolates through wrapping paper, so keep edible gifts up high until Christmas morning.
- **Water in a real tree's stand**, which some pets will drink. Cover it if you can.

Anchoring the tree to a wall hook or heavy base also helps if you have a cat that climbs or a big dog with a happy tail.

## Visitor questions

### How do I stop the dog getting out when the house is full?

This is the one that worries me most, because gates get left open when people are carrying eskies and salads. A few practical things:

- **Put a sign on the side gate** asking people to close it behind them.
- **Give your pet a quiet room** with water, a bed and a closed door during the busiest part of the day.
- **Make sure their ID is current before the day.** Check the details on their collar tag are correct and legible, and that their microchip registration has your current phone number. If you're not sure what should go on a tag, I covered it in [what to engrave on a pet tag](/posts/personalised-pet-tags-what-to-engrave/), and the [tag text checker](/tag-text-checker/) will tell you if your wording fits.

### Should I let guests feed them?

Ask everyone not to, and say it early. One firm, friendly line at the door works better than chasing down three cousins later. Kids especially love sneaking food to the dog, so give them a jar of the dog's own treats and let that be "their job".

## Summer questions

### It's 34 degrees on Christmas Day. What changes?

Plenty. In much of Australia, Christmas Day is hot, and pets can overheat quickly, especially flat-faced breeds, older pets and heavy-coated dogs.

- **Never leave a pet in a parked car**, even "just for a minute" while you run back in for the forgotten trifle. Cars heat up very quickly.
- **Walk early or late.** Hot footpaths and roads can burn paws. Put the back of your hand on the surface for a few seconds; if it's too hot for you, it's too hot for them.
- **Shade and water everywhere.** More than one water bowl, in spots that stay shaded as the sun moves.
- **Know the signs of heat stress**, like heavy panting, drooling, weakness or collapse, and treat it as an emergency.

### Anything specific to Queensland in summer?

Two things worth mentioning for us in the south-east. **Cane toads** are more active in warm, wet weather, and a dog that mouths one can become very unwell quickly; wipe the gums with a damp cloth, away from the throat, and call the vet. And **paralysis ticks** are a real risk along the east coast, so keep up with your tick prevention and check your dog after bush walks or holidays in coastal areas.

## Fireworks and storms

### New Year's Eve fireworks terrify my dog. What can I do now?

Start planning now, not on 31 December. Talk to your vet well ahead of time if your pet has had severe reactions before; they can talk through options. In the meantime:

- **Keep them inside** in a familiar room during fireworks, with curtains closed and some background noise like the TV.
- **Secure the yard.** Frightened dogs can jump or dig under fences they'd never normally attempt.
- **Double-check that ID tag and microchip** again. Many animals go missing around fireworks and summer storms, and a tag that can be read in seconds is the fastest way home.

## The short version

- Keep the vet's and emergency vet's numbers in your phone.
- No cooked bones, ham fat, chocolate, grapes or dried fruit, macadamias, onions, xylitol or alcohol.
- No lilies in a house with cats, and no tinsel for cats who play with it.
- Close the gate, give pets a quiet room, and check their ID details before the visitors arrive.
- Plan for heat, and plan for fireworks.

None of this means pets can't enjoy Christmas. A long walk in the cool of the morning, a new toy, and a safe, quiet spot while the humans eat too much is a pretty good day for most dogs and cats.
